Castle Hill from about 10:00, before the crowds, then north for roses and an Ottoman bath. The evening is left open for friends.

Fisherman’s Bastion
Must-see ▾
History Built between 1895 and 1902 by Frigyes Schulek for Hungary’s millennium celebrations. It looks medieval but never defended anything: the name comes from the fishermen’s guild that guarded this stretch of the old walls.
Did you know The seven turrets stand for the seven Magyar tribes that arrived in the Carpathian Basin in 896.
The practical bit Get there by 10:00; it only gets busier. The Parliament is straight across the river, so this is the photo stop.
📍 Take me there
Mary Magdalene Tower
▾
History The church was built in the 13th century and was the only functioning Christian church in Hungary during the Ottoman occupation, until the Turks made it a mosque. Franz I was crowned here in 1792. Wrecked in the 1944 to 1945 siege, the rest of the church was demolished under communism; only the tower was left standing.
The practical bit Around 170 steps to the top for a view over Castle Hill and the Buda Hills. Check the opening hours on the day.
📍 Take me there
Tóth Árpád sétány
▾
Why it matters The tree-lined western rampart of Castle Hill, looking out over the Buda Hills rather than the river. In October the leaves turn, and it stays quiet even when the Bastion is packed.
The practical bit Walk it south from the Magdalene Tower towards the palace. Free.
📍 Take me there
Buda Castle Terraces
▾
History The royal palace has been destroyed and rebuilt many times. It was left largely in ruins by the 1944 to 1945 siege and rebuilt afterwards, and today it houses the Hungarian National Gallery and the National Széchényi Library.
Did you know The bronze Turul on the gate by the funicular is the mythical bird of Hungarian origin legends, said to have guided the Magyars to their homeland.
The practical bit The terraces are free. Walk down afterwards to lunch at Déryné on Krisztina tér, then tram 4 or 6 from Széll Kálmán tér to the Buda end of Margaret Bridge for Gül Baba.

Gül Baba’s Tomb and Rose Garden
▾
History Built between 1543 and 1548 for Gül Baba, an Ottoman dervish poet whose name means Father of Roses. It is the northernmost Islamic pilgrimage site in the world, restored in 2018 with terraced rose and lavender gardens looking over the Danube.
Did you know Rózsadomb, Rose Hill, the whole district around it, is named after the roses he is said to have planted.
The practical bit Free. Open 10:00 to 18:00. Entrance at Türbe tér 1, up from the Buda end of Margaret Bridge.

Veli Bej Bath
Hidden gem ▾
Why it matters Built in 1574 to 1575 under Sokollu Mustafa Pasha, forgotten for centuries, restored in 2012. The octagonal pool under the Ottoman dome is the real thing, and the bath caps at 80 people, which is why it feels private. With Gellért closed and Király shut since 2019, only this and Rudas still run as Ottoman baths in Buda.
The practical bit Friday hours: 06:00 to 12:00 and 15:00 to 21:00. Be there at 15:00, when the water is fresh and the room is quiet: the midday break is a full water change. Three-hour ticket around 5,700 to 7,200 HUF (about £14 to £18). Árpád fejedelem útja 7, a short walk down from Gül Baba.

Faust Wine Cellar
▾
History The cellar is part of the old labyrinth under the Castle District. You reach it through the medieval Dominican cloister inside the Hilton on Hess András tér, then 54 steps down into the rock.
Did you know The tastings pour from across Hungary’s 22 wine regions, family and artisan wineries whose bottles are hard to buy anywhere else, finished with a glass of pálinka.
The practical bit Tastings at 14:00 and 17:00. Closed Tuesday and Wednesday. Book the 14:00 tasting: it leads straight up to White Raven in the same building.

White Raven Skybar (If Open)
▾
Why it matters The rooftop of the Hilton, right next to Matthias Church and Fisherman’s Bastion, with the Parliament and Margaret Island laid out below. A rare angle on the whole city from the top of the Castle District.
Did you know The sun sets behind the Buda Hills, not over the Danube, so this is a view bar rather than a sunset bar. The statue walk below does sunset better.
The practical bit Seasonal: it closes for winter with no fixed date, and hours shorten in autumn, so check whiteravenskybar.com that morning. Same building as Faust: lobby lifts to the 6th floor, then a second lift. Signature cocktails 6,900 HUF (about £17), classics 5,900 HUF (about £14). Smart casual, no trainers or flip-flops.

Leo Rooftop (Plan B for White Raven)
▾
Why it matters On the 8th floor of Hotel Clark, exactly where the Chain Bridge lands in Buda, looking over Buda Castle, the Chain Bridge, Gresham Palace and the Danube. It is named after the stone lions that guard the bridge, and it stays open all year.
Did you know The Parliament is mostly hidden from here: the show is the castle and the bridge. You get the Parliament from your own roof anyway.

Prince Buda and Princess Pest
▾
History Officially the Kilátókő-szobor, the Lookout Rock statue, by Márta Lesenyei, installed in 1982. The two figures, Buda and Pest, reach towards each other across the river: the city’s two halves, joined into one Budapest in 1873.
Did you know It is stone and bronze, 150 cm tall, and the narrow gap between the two stones is the Danube.
The practical bit From Castle Hill, walk down through Tabán to Döbrentei tér (about 30 minutes), then up the path past the Garden of Philosophers (about 20 minutes, with stairs). Be there for sunset, around 17:53. From Leo instead, it is about 15 minutes along the river to Döbrentei tér. The Citadella above reopened in April 2026 with a new park if you have legs left. Free. Rudas is at the bottom of the same path.

Suggested for Sunday

A tram to the hills, a train run by children, a chairlift down, the river by boat, and back on the roof for sunset. Start around 10:00, and keep the night short: the ride is at 05:30.

Children’s Railway
▾
History Built from 1948 as the Pioneer Railway, part of the communist youth movement. Children aged 10 to 14 have staffed it ever since, selling tickets, signalling and waving trains through; only the drivers are adults.
Did you know At about 11 km through the Buda Hills it is the world’s longest children’s railway, and it runs almost every day of the year.
The practical bit Tram 61 from Széll Kálmán tér to Hűvösvölgy, the end of the line. Ride the railway up to János-hegy station, walk to the Elisabeth Lookout, then take the chairlift down. Tickets at the station.
📍 Take me there
Zugliget Chairlift (Libegő)
Must-see ▾
History Hungary’s first passenger ropeway, opened on 20 August 1970. It climbs 262 metres over 1,040 metres of track to János-hegy, the highest point in Budapest, in about 12 minutes each way.
Did you know The name came from a public competition. The winning entry was lebegő, meaning floating, which became libegő. Rejected ideas included Hegyi-kopter and Űrbusz, space bus.
The practical bit Closed for maintenance until 16 October, running again from Saturday 17 October, 10:00 to 17:00. Today you ride it down: one way 3,000 HUF (about £7), in BudapestGO. The upper station is a short walk from the Elisabeth Lookout (Erzsébet-kilátó) on János-hegy, the highest point in the city.
📍 Take me there
Late Lunch at Émile
▾
History A restored villa in the leafy 2nd district run by the Gerbeaud group, the same house behind the grand café on Vörösmarty tér since 1858. Locals call it Buda’s answer to Gerbeaud.
Did you know You walk in over a glass floor: the cellar with more than 130 wines sits directly under your feet.
The practical bit Orló utca 1, 1026. Check Sunday opening hours before you go. Late lunch after the chairlift, a short ride from its lower station. Villa, garden and a cake counter from the Gerbeaud house; mains around 7,500 HUF (about £18) on the current menu.
📍 Take me there
The Danube from the Water
▾
Why it matters The city’s own BKV boat loops Kossuth tér, Margaret Island, Batthyány tér, Várkert Bazár and Petőfi tér in about an hour. It is the only seat in Budapest with Buda and Pest both in front of you.
The practical bit Board at Batthyány tér, a short walk from the flat, and ride the loop to Kossuth tér on the Pest side. In 2026 the boats run hourly, typically in the afternoons, with the last full loop leaving Kossuth tér around 17:00. The winter 2026 day pass was 2,800 HUF (about £7). Tickets at the pier or at bkvhajo.jegy.eu; boats carry about 100 people and a very low Danube can suspend the service, so check that morning.
📍 Take me there
Szabadság tér and the Postal Savings Bank
▾
History Liberty Square holds Hungary’s arguments in stone: the last Soviet army memorial still standing in central Budapest, a Ronald Reagan statue from 2011, and the contested 2014 memorial to the victims of the German occupation, with a protesters’ memorial of photos and letters facing it.
Did you know The old Exchange Palace on the square played Harrison Ford’s hideout in Blade Runner 2049. Round the corner on Hold utca, Ödön Lechner’s 1901 Postal Savings Bank is covered in colourful Zsolnay majolica tiles and folk motifs.
The practical bit About five minutes on foot from the Kossuth tér pier. Free. Then the M2 from Kossuth Lajos tér, one stop to Batthyány tér, and home for sunset.

Good to Know

The small things that make the weekend smoother, and the rooftops worth the climb.

Paprikás csirke with nokedli. Chicken paprikash with small soft dumplings and a sour-cream sauce. The Sunday lunch of the whole country.
Túrós csusza. Noodles with túró (fresh curd cheese), sour cream and crisp bacon. Deeply unglamorous, deeply good, rarely ordered by visitors.
Főzelék. A thick vegetable stew (pea, lentil or squash) that Hungarians eat as a main. Order it once to understand home cooking here.
Somlói galuska for two. Layers of sponge, walnuts, chocolate sauce and whipped cream. One spoon each is a waste; one bowl, two spoons is the point.
Pálinka before, Tokaji after. A small fruit brandy (apricot is barack, plum is szilva) opens the meal; a glass of sweet Tokaji Aszú closes it.
Eye contact on every toast. Look at each person as the glasses meet. Looking away reads as careless at best.
Do not clink beer glasses. Wine and pálinka, clink away. Beer, raise it and say the word.
Szia for friends, Jó napot for shops. Szia is hello and goodbye among people you know. With friends, it is right from the first second.
Name days count. Hungarians celebrate their névnap, the saint’s day of their first name, almost like a small birthday. Expect flowers, cake and a round of Egészségedre.
Check the bill for szervizdíj. Many restaurants add a service charge of 10 to 15 percent. If it is there, you do not add a tip on top.
Gellért is closed until at least 2028. The whole complex shut on 1 October 2025 for renovation. Rudas and Veli Bej are the plan instead.
The chairlift reopens only on 17 October. It is closed for maintenance from 28 September to 16 October, so Thursday and Friday are out. Saturday or Sunday only.
Rudas night tickets cannot be bought at the door. Online only. Turn up without one and you are going home dry.
Veli Bej shuts from 12:00 to 15:00 and caps at 80 people. If it is full you get a number and wait in the café. Opening time at 15:00 is the sweet spot.
Outdoor-only rooftops go quiet in October. Many close from around October to April. White Raven on the Hilton is seasonal and closes for winter without a fixed date, so check its website before walking up.
🍷 Drink Hungarian, not French. Hungary has 22 wine regions and most of the good bottles never leave the country. Faust, Cabrio and Borkonyha all pour local first. Order a Hungarian glass everywhere and the weekend doubles as a wine tour.
🫖 The Ottoman baths were built for hours, not photos. Rudas and Veli Bej are 16th-century rooms designed for slowing down. Leave the phones in the locker and stay longer than feels reasonable.
